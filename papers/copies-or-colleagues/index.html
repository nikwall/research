Copies or Colleagues? Shared Error among AI Models and Human Forecasters

Niklas Wallmeier

Working paper

Version 1, November 2026. A reply to Imas, Koh and Legg (2026), “Organizing Intelligence”. Code and data to replicate every number are on GitHub.

PDFCode and data (GitHub)Summary (2 pages)SlidesImas, Koh and Legg (2026)Cite

In short

Can a team of people beat a better AI because people err in different directions? On a forecasting benchmark, AI models err far more alike than people, even net of luck, and people kept the lead only where markets and discussing teams pooled what they know.

What we find

  • Forecasting puts people and AI on the same questions before anyone knows the answer. The test uses 578 questions answered in 2024 by 500 members of the public, 40 superforecasters and 17 AI models, and 1.7 million forecasts by 134 models up to October 2026.
  • AI models make the same mistakes. Models from different developers share 86 percent of their squared error with one another, members of the public 70 percent. Part of that is luck in the outcome, which every forecaster shares. Even after removing the largest possible role of luck, models share at least 73 percent of their deviations from the truth, and the public at least 42 percent.
  • Mixing the best models no longer pays. In the second half of 2026, averaging the five best models improves on the best one by less than 1 percent.
  • The tool-using systems that reached parity with superforecasters in 2026 are more accurate: the entries of the three teams named in the parity report beat the best plain model by 7 percent. Averaging the two adds little, and on market questions they match the price without beating it. Whether they make different mistakes, the data cannot tell yet.
  • Variety alone buys little: no crowd of laypeople, however large, beat a 2024 model on questions about data series. People kept the lead where they were organized, in a superforecaster team that discussed its forecasts and in prediction markets.
